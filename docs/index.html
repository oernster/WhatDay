What fucking day is it?

Everything tells you the time and date; nothing tells you the day. WhatDay does. It puts the name of the day in a small dark strip above your taskbar, changes it at midnight and does absolutely nothing else.

Born of frustration

The clock knows the year. It will not tell you it is Thursday.

You work from home. Or you have just had a week off. Or it has simply been one of those weeks, where Tuesday and Friday are the same grey shape. You glance at the corner of the screen and Windows helpfully tells you the hour, the minute and the date down to the year.

You then count on your fingers from the last thing you are sure happened on a Monday.

That is the whole problem. A computer that can render a film, translate Portuguese and lose an argument with a printer could not be persuaded to show one word. So now it does.

Engineered far beyond reason

It shows one word from a list of seven. Here is what that took.

A reasonable person would have written this in an afternoon. What happened instead is below. Every number on this page was measured from the code, because making them up would have been the one shortcut out of character.

146,097

Days checked: every date from 2000 to 2399, each against a second, independent weekday formula written into the tests.

In case the programming language got Tuesdays wrong.
598

Time zones walked midnight by midnight for an entire century, to prove every day begins exactly when it should.

You live in one of them.
746

Midnights that never happen, where clocks jump straight past 00:00. WhatDay finds when those days really start, by bisection, to the nanosecond.

A nanosecond is quite precise for a word.
100%

Test coverage of every line that decides what day it is. The build refuses to run if it slips.

There is no switch to skip it. That was deliberate.
10

Structural tests that fail the build if the architecture of this one-word program is compromised.

Four layers. Clean architecture. For "Saturday".
600+

Lines of formal requirements specification, every requirement tied to the test that proves it. Every change of mind is a numbered amendment.

One of them is about the Quit button.
2

Colour-science standards (WCAG contrast and CIEDE2000) consulted so six colours are legible and look nothing alike.

Blue and Purple were closest. They were checked.
1 : 1

Roughly as many lines of tests as lines of program.

Over a hundred tests, for seven possible outputs.
5

Routes through its own bespoke installer: install, update, go back, repair and uninstall, each with its own screen.

It has a repair screen. For a word.
0

Network connections. Not "we promise": a test fails the build if any of its code so much as imports a network package.

It does not need the internet to know it is Wednesday.

Is it overkill? Yes. Is it ever wrong about the day? Also, demonstrably, no.

The feature list

Short. Deliberately.

What it does

  • Shows the day, in English, in full, in a strip as tall as your taskbar.
  • Changes at midnight in whatever timezone Windows is set to. Fly to Sydney and the day follows you.
  • Catches up after sleep, a clock change or a timezone change.
  • Stays where you drag it across restarts. Never on the taskbar.
  • Hides for fullscreen films and anything else that takes the whole screen, as the taskbar does.
  • Starts when you sign in, if you leave that ticked in setup. Quit it from the tray whenever you like.

What it does not do

  • The date, the time or a week number. Your clock has those. It is very proud of them.
  • Light mode. The strip is always dark. So is this page. You were warned.
  • Settings windows. The tray menu holds About, Support, six colours and Quit. That is the lot.
  • The internet. No updates, no telemetry, no accounts. It sends nothing, ever.
  • Other languages. The days are in English, wherever you are.
  • Anything but Windows. Windows only, for good.

Install

Install it once. Never wonder again.

  1. Download WhatDaySetup.exe from the latest release.
  2. Run it and choose Install. It installs for your account only, so Windows never asks for administrator rights.
  3. Look down. There is the day. Leave Start WhatDay when I sign in to Windows ticked and it will be there every time.

Runs on Windows 10 or later. It is built and tested on Windows 11, where the strip gets its translucent Acrylic backdrop (22H2 and later); how it looks on older versions has not been checked. To remove it, uninstall from Settings > Apps; that takes everything it wrote with it: the program, its settings and its log.
